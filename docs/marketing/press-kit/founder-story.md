# Founder Story — Why Lyfos exists

A few years ago, a close friend died unexpectedly. He was the family's
youngest, the one everyone leaned on, the one who handled the bills, the
investments, the insurance, the mobile recharges, the OTPs. He died
without a will and without any system for what came next.

For the next six months, his sister became a forensic accountant of his
own life. She found a bank account in Bangalore he'd opened in college.
She found a health insurance policy he was paying for that none of them
knew about. She did not find the SIP that I knew he had — that one is
still untraced. She did not find the password to his email — the one
his bank used for OTPs — and so the family had to swear affidavits in
court to reset it, three months after the funeral.

What I noticed, watching this from the next room, was that the problem
wasn't a missing folder. He had folders. He had multiple. He had
spreadsheets. The problem was that those folders were locked behind a
device, a password, a 2FA app, and a series of recovery questions that
he had answered as a joke nobody else would understand. The folders
existed, and were useless.

I started looking for a product that solved this. Password managers
weren't designed for it — they trust one user and treat death as an
edge case. Estate-planning tools were designed for wills and trusts,
not for the messy digital exhaust of a modern life. Some "digital legacy"
services existed but were either trust-me cloud services that asked you
to upload your secrets in plaintext, or required your nominee to send
in a notarised death certificate plus a passport-size photo plus an
Aadhaar copy at the worst week of their life.

None of them passed what I came to think of as the grandma test: can
the person who has to use this, on the worst day of their life, with
shaking hands at 11 pm on a Sunday, actually use it?

So I started building Lyfos in 2026, alone, full-time, with the
following design constraints written above my desk:

  1. We cannot read your vault. Not for support, not for a subpoena,
     not for the founder. The architecture has to prevent it.
  2. Your nominee should not need a notary. The trust comes from the
     three of five people you already chose.
  3. Your living self has to be protected. If a release ever starts
     while you're still here, you get fourteen days and four channels
     of alerts to abort. One tap, no questions.
  4. If we disappear, your vault still works. Local-first. Always
     a copy. Your recovery phrase decrypts even if Lyfos is gone.
  5. It has to be cheap enough that the people who most need it can
     afford it. ₹999 a year is the line we drew.

The product is bootstrapped. I have not raised money. The reason is
simple: a vault is an unusual category of product because it has to
survive its company. Investors push for growth at any cost; the
product I'm building requires patience, modesty, and the willingness
to leave money on the table when an option would compromise the trust.
Bootstrap is the only structure that aligns my incentives with my
users for the next ten years.

I think about my friend often. The whole reason Lyfos exists is so that
the next family doesn't have to do what his did.

— Founder, Lyfos
